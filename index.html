Beginner's Guide to Adobe Photoshop

A friendly reference for when you're stuck — explore the tabs below, follow the steps, and experiment.

Menu Bar

The Menu Bar is the strip of words at the very top of Photoshop: File, Edit, Image, Layer, Select, Filter, View, Window, Help.

Think of it as your main navigation: nearly every command lives here.

If a menu item is greyed out, it usually means you haven't selected something yet (for example, a layer or a part of the image).

File

Starting & saving work

File > New…
Create a brand new document. Choose the size (e.g. A4, 1920×1080), resolution (300 for print, 72 for screen), and background colour.

File > Open…
Open an existing image or Photoshop file.

File > Save / Save As…
Save your work. Use Save As… to make a copy with a new name or format.

File > Export > Export As…
Export your image as PNG or JPEG (more on this in the Saving & Export tab).

Edit

Fixes & preferences

Edit > Undo / Step Backward
Undo your last actions if you make a mistake.

Edit > Free Transform
Resize, rotate, or flip a layer or selection.

Edit > Preferences (on Windows)
Change general settings like interface colours and performance.

You can also use the History panel (Window > History) to jump back several steps at once.

Image

Size & adjustments

Image > Image Size…
Change how large your image is (in pixels or centimetres).

Image > Canvas Size…
Add extra space around your image or crop it smaller.

Image > Adjustments
Brighten, darken, change colours, etc.
See the Image & Adjustments tab for details.

Filter

Special effects

Filter > Blur
Soften parts of your image (for example, to create a background blur behind text).

Filter > Sharpen
Make edges crisper so the image looks clearer.

Filter > Liquify…
Push and pull pixels as if your image were made of soft clay (use carefully!).

Filters often work on the selected layer only. Make sure you've selected the correct layer in the Layers panel first.
Open any photo and explore each top menu. Hover your mouse over items and read the short descriptions and shortcuts on the right.

Useful Keyboard Shortcuts

Keyboard shortcuts help you work faster and spend less time hunting through menus.

In this guide: Ctrl (Windows) is the same as Cmd on a Mac. So Ctrl+Z on Windows is Cmd+Z on Mac.

Everyday essentials

Undo / Redo last change Ctrl+Z Cmd+Z Toggle between your last two states.
Step backwards through history Ctrl+Alt+Z Cmd+Option+Z Go back more than one step.
Save Ctrl+S Cmd+S Save your current file.
Save As… Ctrl+Shift+S Cmd+Shift+S Save a copy with a different name or format.
New layer Ctrl+Shift+N Cmd+Shift+N Create a new layer above the current one.
Duplicate layer Ctrl+J Cmd+J Copy the current layer.
Free Transform Ctrl+T Cmd+T Resize, rotate, flip, skew etc.

Zoom out Ctrl - Cmd - Zoom further away.
Fit on screen Ctrl+0 Cmd+0 Fit the whole image into your window.

Image Editing (Image > Adjustments)

These adjustments change how your image looks: brightness, contrast, colour, and more.

Find them under: Image > Adjustments.

Brightness / Contrast…

Quick light fix

Brightness

Makes the entire image lighter or darker. Imagine turning up or down a light in the room.

Contrast

Increases or decreases the difference between dark and light areas. Higher contrast = stronger shadows and highlights; lower contrast = flatter, softer image.

If your photo looks dull or “muddy”, try a small boost in Contrast and a tiny increase in Brightness.

Colour Balance…

Fix strange colours

Lets you shift the image towards different colours (for example blue, red, green).

  • Too yellow? Move towards Blue or Cyan.
  • Too cold and blue? Move towards Red or Yellow.
Use the drop-down (Shadows / Midtones / Highlights) to target darker, mid or lighter areas separately.

Hue / Saturation…

Change colour strength

Hue

Changes the actual colours. Drag the slider and watch reds turn to blues, blues to greens, etc. (Fun, but easy to overdo!)

Saturation

Controls how intense the colours are. High saturation = bright, cartoon-like colours. Low saturation = faded or black-and-white.

Lightness

Lightens or darkens the overall image, similar to Brightness.

Extreme saturation can make skin look unnatural and messy. Use small changes first.

Vibrance…

Smart colour boost

Boosts the intensity of dull colours while protecting already bright colours and skin tones.

For people photos, Vibrance is usually safer than Saturation. Try a small Vibrance increase before Saturation.
Open a copy of a photo (so the original is safe). Try:
  1. Use Brightness / Contrast to make it clearer.
  2. Use Colour Balance to fix any strange colour cast.
  3. Add a tiny Vibrance boost to make colours pop.

Layers

Layers are like transparent sheets stacked on top of each other. Everything on the top layer covers layers beneath it.

Open the Layers panel with Window > Layers or press F7.

Create & move layers

Building your stack
  • New layer: Ctrl+Shift+N / Cmd+Shift+N, or click the plus icon at the bottom of the Layers panel.
  • Duplicate a layer: Ctrl+J / Cmd+J (handy before big changes).
  • Move a layer: click and drag the layer up or down in the Layers panel.
Put backgrounds at the bottom, text and important objects higher up.

Hide, lock & group

Stay organised
  • Hide / show layer: click the eye icon next to the layer.
  • Lock layer: click the little lock icon at the top of the Layers panel (stops you from editing it by accident).
  • Group layers: select several layers then press Ctrl+G / Cmd+G, or right-click > Group from Layers.
Use groups for things like “Background”, “Text”, “Effects” to keep your file tidy.

Colour labels

Visual organisation

You can add colours to layers to make them easier to spot:

  • Right-click on a layer.
  • Choose a Colour from the menu.

For example:

Main subject Warning or effects Text Background

Decide your own colour system and stick to it (for example, blue for images, green for text, yellow for guides).

Layer types

What they do
  • Normal layer: standard image content, pixels you can paint on or erase.
  • Type layer: created when you use the Text Tool. Always use this for editable text.
  • Adjustment layer: special layer that applies effects like brightness or colour without permanently changing the pixels underneath.
Use adjustment layers instead of direct Image > Adjustments when you want to stay non-destructive.
Make a mini poster:
  1. Create a background colour on one layer.
  2. Add a title on a text layer.
  3. Paste or place an image on another layer.
  4. Colour-code each layer and group them.
  5. Practise turning layers on and off with the eye icons.

Saving & Exporting

There are two big ideas here: working files (PSD) and finished images (PNG/JPEG).

Saving as PSD

Working file

A .PSD file keeps all your layers, text, and adjustment layers so you can come back and edit everything later.

  1. Go to File > Save As…
  2. Choose a folder.
  3. In Format, choose Photoshop (*.PSD).
  4. Click Save.
Always keep a PSD version of important projects. Think of it as your master copy.

Export as PNG

Transparent / high quality

PNG is good for logos, graphics, or anything that needs a transparent background or sharp edges.

  1. Hide any background layer if you want transparency (turn off the eye icon).
  2. Go to File > Export > Export As…
  3. Choose PNG as the format.
  4. Check that Transparency is ticked if you need it.
  5. Click Export and choose a folder.

Export as JPEG

Photos & sharing

JPEGs are great for photographs and when you want a smaller file size (for example, uploading to a website).

  1. Go to File > Export > Export As…
  2. Choose JPEG as the format.
  3. Use the Quality slider:
    • 80–100: very high quality, bigger file.
    • 60–80: good quality, smaller file (often fine for web).
  4. Click Export and choose a folder.
JPEG does not support transparency. Transparent areas will turn solid (usually white).

Save for Web (Legacy)

Fine control

Some versions of Photoshop also have: File > Export > Save for Web (Legacy)…

This gives detailed control over file size and previewing how your image will look on the web.

  • Choose JPEG or PNG.
  • Use the quality options to balance size and appearance.
  • Watch the estimated file size in the bottom-left of the preview window.
Save one project three ways: PSD, PNG and JPEG. Compare:
  • Which one keeps your layers?
  • How do the file sizes differ?
  • Which would you use for printing? For a website?
